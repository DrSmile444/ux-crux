# Trust: platform-agnostic onboarding, permissions, and destructive-action safety

See `../shared/evidence-model.md` and `severity-model.md` for how to report findings from these rules.

## Onboarding & trust (platform-agnostic half)

| ID | Rule | Evidence | Default severity | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| O01 | Skip standalone onboarding when the interface can be understood through normal use. | Strong | Moderate | Apple HIG Onboarding; NN/g Mobile-App Onboarding: An Analysis of Components and Techniques |
| O02 | If onboarding is necessary, keep it fast, optional where possible, and focused on value/setup that cannot be learned contextually. | Strong | Major | Apple HIG Onboarding; NN/g Mobile-App Onboarding |
| O03 | Teach through interaction or contextual tips rather than requiring users to memorize a feature tour. | Strong | Moderate | Apple HIG Onboarding; NN/g Mobile-App Onboarding |
| O06 | Permission denial gracefully degrades the experience and provides a path to enable access later; denial is not treated as user error. | Platform contract | Major | Android Request runtime permissions; NN/g 3 Design Considerations for Effective Mobile-App Permission Requests |
| O07 | Collect/request only data and permissions actually required for the product/feature. | Strong | Blocker | Apple HIG Privacy; Android Request runtime permissions |
| O11 | Login/registration asks for the minimum information and explains the benefit; guest/anonymous use is offered when the core experience does not require identity. | Contextual | Major | NN/g A Checklist for Registration and Login Forms on Mobile, Login Walls Stop Users in Their Tracks |
| O12 | Privacy-sensitive history (for example search history) is displayed cautiously and can be cleared where appropriate. | Strong | Moderate | Apple HIG Searching, Privacy |
| O13 | When a form collects gender, sex, or other identity/demographic data, gender identity, sex assigned at birth, and pronouns are treated as distinct fields when more than one is actually needed (not one forced binary choice), each such field is optional or offers a free-text alternative where possible, and the form states in context why the data is collected and how it affects the product (for example billing eligibility, a biometric calculation, or social display). This is narrower than O07's general minimum-data-collection principle: O07 asks whether the data should be collected at all, O13 asks — once collection is justified — whether the fields are structured and explained transparently. | Contextual | Major | Apple HIG Privacy, Inclusion |
| O14 | A free trial does not require payment-method entry before it begins. When a trial converts to a paid plan, the user receives clear advance notice before the conversion charge, and cancellation — before or after conversion — is available fully online and self-service, without requiring phone support or comparable friction. Distinct from `psychology`'s PT04 (exit-friction parity in general): this rule is specifically about payment-method-collection timing for trials, not overall cancellation-vs-signup friction symmetry. | Contextual | Major | Falbe, Frederiksen & Andersen, *White Hat UX* (Forced Continuity / No Strings Attached patterns) |
| O15 | A mobile web page does not block its content behind a full-screen "install our app" interstitial; a non-intrusive contextual banner (a modest top or bottom bar) is the acceptable way to promote a native app. | Strong | Major | Falbe, Frederiksen & Andersen, *White Hat UX* (Door Slam pattern) |
| O16 | A feature that publishes or broadcasts a user's activity from one context (a third-party site, a private group, a prior audience) to a different, broader, or unrelated audience by default requires an explicit, active, persistent opt-in choice made before anything is shared — never only a passive, easily-missed, or auto-expiring opt-out notice. | Strong | Blocker | Andrew Hinton, *Understanding Context* (Facebook Beacon / context-collapse case) |
| O17 | A messaging, posting, or reply interface makes the public-versus-private destination of the content clearly and unambiguously visible immediately adjacent to the send/submit control, whenever the layout could plausibly cause a user to mistake a public-facing field for a private one (or vice versa). | Strong | Major | danah boyd, networked-publics properties (persistence, replicability, scalability, searchability) |
| O18 | A storage or sync-toggle warning that tells the user files will be affected states where the affected files actually live (locally, in the cloud, or both) and what happens specifically to any local copy, rather than using vague "will be removed" language with no location context. | Strong | Major | Andrew Hinton, *Understanding Context* (foggy cloud-metaphor / iCloud deletion-warning case) |

## Cost & commitment transparency

| ID | Rule | Evidence | Default severity | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CT01 | A mandatory fee or cost that will apply to a priced flow (a checkout, subscription signup, or service enrollment) is disclosed at the earliest point where the flow's total cost can be meaningfully communicated, not for the first time at the final commitment step. A cost component that genuinely cannot be calculated until required information is provided (e.g. location-specific tax needing a shipping address) is a legitimate exception, provided baseline costs are still disclosed early. This rule is deliberately general — it does not itself specify e-commerce-specific mechanics such as cart architecture or faceted search. | Strong | Major | Falbe, Frederiksen & Andersen, *White Hat UX* (Hidden Costs pattern, generalized) |
| CT02 | A struck-through or "was" price shown beside a current price is a former price at which the item was actually offered to the public on a regular basis for a reasonably substantial period, not an inflated figure created to make a discount look larger. Distinct from PB03, which concerns anchors hiding total cost; CT02 tests the authenticity of the reference price. Does not apply to a reference price labelled with a verifiable basis (for example a manufacturer's suggested price); price history is not visible in a screenshot, so report `NOT ASSESSABLE` unless the evidence shows the contradiction. | Strong | Major | U.S. Federal Trade Commission, Guides Against Deceptive Pricing, 16 CFR 233.1 (former price comparisons) |
| CT03 | A product page gives an estimated shipping cost (a flat rate, a location-based estimate, or a transparent range) so the user can judge the total order cost before adding to the cart, and links or summarizes the return policy in plain language. Distinct from CT01, which concerns when mandatory fees are disclosed in a priced flow. Does not apply to digital goods without delivery or returns; information the evidence does not show is `NOT ASSESSABLE`. | Strong | Moderate | Baymard Institute, Holst, "Product Pages Need to Show 'Estimated Shipping Costs' (Yet 43% of Sites Don't)" (2017); Baymard Institute guideline "Return Policy Discoverability" (#803); via Baymard Institute, Christian Holst, "15 UX Best Practices for Creating User-Friendly E-Commerce Product Pages" |
| CT04 | The card and payment fields are visually contained with a style used only for them (a border, a background, shading, or a security badge or padlock within the area), so users perceive the payment area as more secure than the rest of the form; the cue is a perception cue only and any badge or security text shown must describe protection the site actually has. Distinct from CT01, which concerns when mandatory costs are disclosed. Does not apply to a payment sheet handled by the operating system or a hosted wallet; the real technical security of the page is `NOT ASSESSABLE`. | Strong | Moderate | Baymard Institute, Jamie Holst, "How Users Perceive Security During the Checkout Flow (Incl. New 'Trust Seal' Study 2023)" (2016); Baymard Institute, Jamie Holst, "Visually Reinforce Your Credit Card Fields (89% Get it Wrong)" (2012); Baymard Institute, Christian Holst, "Customers Perceive Only Parts of a Checkout-page as Being Secure" (2010) |
| CT05 | After a cancellation request the order page shows a persistent "Cancellation Requested" order state (not a temporary overlay) that states the expected timing of the outcome and the payment impact, avoids vague wording such as "we will do our best", and leaves no contradicting older status on the page. Distinct from PR01, which concerns the gap between on-screen completion and the goal being realized in general, not the order-state wording after a cancellation. Does not apply to a site that processes every cancellation instantly and shows the result at once; the content of confirmation emails when only the screen is shown is `NOT ASSESSABLE`. | Strong | Moderate | Baymard Institute, Christian Holst, "Order Cancellation Request: Have a 'Cancellation Requested' Order State" (2018) |
| CT06 | Order tracking stays on the site itself: the order-tracking page shows the expected delivery date, a status progress bar, the carrier name, a linked tracking number, a shipping history and a package summary instead of sending users to a third-party site; when a return starts the order status updates at once, and the return label is saved for later (for example emailed, with a message saying so) and not offered only as a print step. Distinct from CT05, which concerns the order state after a cancellation request, not tracking details or return status. Does not apply to digital goods and sites with no shipments or returns; carrier data accuracy and the content of notification emails is `NOT ASSESSABLE`. | Strong | Moderate | Baymard Institute, Sally Collins, "Always Provide 6 Key Order-Tracking Details on the Ecommerce Site" (2019, updated 2026); Baymard Institute, Christian Holst, "The 'Order Returns' Experience is Critical for Customer Retention — Yet 54% of Sites Have a Returns Interface with Substantial UX Issues" (2019) |
| CT07 | Each shipping option in checkout shows its estimated delivery date next to its cost (not only a shipping speed or name); all fulfilment options the site offers, including store pickup and local delivery, are listed and switchable in the checkout fulfilment step; and a free-shipping offer also appears near the buy section of the product page, not only in a banner or header. Distinct from CT03, which concerns an estimated shipping cost and a return policy summary before add to cart, not delivery dates, fulfilment switching or the placement of free-shipping notices. Does not apply to sites with a single shipping option, and a free-shipping offer that has a condition the user does not meet; delivery-date accuracy, which a screenshot cannot show is `NOT ASSESSABLE`. | Strong | Moderate | Baymard Institute, Iva Olah, "Use “Delivery Date” Not “Shipping Speed” (41% Don’t) — From UX Research to Implementation Roadmap" (2023); Baymard Institute, Edward Scott, "Include All Order-Fulfillment Options in the Fulfillment-Selector Interface (50% Don’t)" (2023); Baymard Institute, Edward Scott, "Product Pages: ‘Free Shipping’ Should Not Only Be in a Site-Wide Banner (32% Get It Wrong)" (2017) |
| CT08 | The site footer (or the mobile footer menu) has direct links to Return Policy and Shipping Info, using those plain keywords and not only a generic FAQ or Help link; where returns are offered in store, the return interface or help section presents in-store return as an equal option beside return by mail and not as a small note. Distinct from CT03, which concerns shipping cost and a return policy summary on the product page, not footer links or the in-store return option. Does not apply to sites with no returns, or with no stores for in-store return; the return policy terms themselves is `NOT ASSESSABLE`. | Strong | Minor | Baymard Institute, Christian Holst, "Have Direct Links to ‘Return Policy’ and ‘Shipping Info’ in the Footer (20% don’t)" (2019, updated 2025); Baymard Institute, Edward Scott, "Self-Service UX: Promote In-Store Returns Alongside Mailed Return Options" (2018, updated 2025) |

## Destructive-action safety (trust-relevant half of Errors & recovery)

| ID | Rule | Evidence | Default severity | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| E05 | Destructive or financial consequences are clearly visible before commitment; cost/irreversibility is not hidden in secondary text. | Strong | Blocker | NN/g Confirmation Dialogs Can Prevent User Errors; Apple HIG Alerts |
| E07 | Back/cancel during editing cannot silently discard meaningful work; save draft, warn only when necessary, or offer undo/recovery. | Strong | Blocker | Android Core app quality guidelines; Apple HIG Design principles, Alerts |
| E08 | For a high-stakes or security-sensitive action (a privacy/security scan, a large financial transfer, an irreversible account-level change), a brief staged process or perceptible "checking"/"scanning" step can serve as a legitimate trust signal, distinct from E05/E07's purpose of preventing accidental loss. This is a narrow, justified exception, not a general license to add delay: an unexplained or artificial delay on a routine, low-stakes, or frequently repeated action is itself a finding, not a trust-building technique. | Contextual | Moderate | NN/g Trust and Credibility in UX; NN/g Website Response Times |

The general error-prevention/recovery rules (state preservation on failure, idempotent retries, recovering without redoing unrelated work) are covered by the separate `usability` skill, not repeated here. This file covers only the confirmation-and-consent angle of destructive actions, plus (E08) the narrower question of deliberate friction as a trust signal.

## Post-interaction reassurance

| ID | Rule | Evidence | Default severity | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| PR01 | A transaction, booking, submission, or other commitment flow accounts for what happens in the gap after the user leaves the reviewed screen — confirmation messaging, a status update, or another reassurance mechanism during any silent wait before the user's goal is fully realized (before a booking is confirmed, a delivery arrives, or a request is fulfilled) — not only the on-screen completion state shown at the moment of submission. This is distinct from `usability`'s S06/S07, which govern in-product empty/loading/error states visible while the user is still looking at the screen; PR01 covers the reassurance gap after the user has left the screen entirely. Does not apply when the transaction completes fully and immediately with no subsequent waiting period. | Strong | Moderate | Mark Wells, *User Experience Design* (end-to-end scenario and pinch-point mapping) |
| PR02 | A background protective or maintenance system that works silently (a security scan, an automatic backup, threat/ad blocking) surfaces a periodic, non-intrusive summary of its activity ("12 threats blocked this week"), rather than giving no indication of what it has done. Users read unexplained silence as proof the protection was unnecessary and are more likely to disable it, undermining the protection's own purpose. Distinct from `usability`'s S08, which discourages *noisy* confirmation of trivial, user-initiated actions — the opposite failure mode: PR02 concerns invisible, ongoing protective work the user did not directly initiate, not a routine action the user just performed. | Strong | Moderate | Don Norman, *Design for a Better World* (invisibility of prevention; safety-success backlash — COVID-precaution backlash case; SCUBA-instructor praise for a dropped weight belt) |

## Reservoir of Goodwill

| ID | Rule | Evidence | Default severity | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GW01 | Interface friction is evaluated against a "reservoir of goodwill" model: every friction point depletes a user's situational tolerance, and depletion — not any single failure alone — drives abandonment or complaint. Flag, as goodwill depletors: hiding information the user needs to complete or trust a task (support contact details, shipping rates, or prices buried behind extra clicks with no stated reason); unnecessary or disproportionate data requests for a simple task; hollow "shucking and jiving" copy that mimics sincerity without conveying substantive information (for example "Your call is important to us" with no actionable content); promotional material ("sizzle") positioned to block or delay immediate task completion; and amateurish or unprofessional execution that undermines confidence the product will work correctly. Distinct from CT01, which addresses the timing of mandatory-cost disclosure specifically within priced flows, not this broader taxonomy of information-hiding and low-effort execution. | Strong | Major | Steve Krug, *Don't Make Me Think, Revisited* (Reservoir of Goodwill model and goodwill depletors) |
| GW02 | A form field does not reject a validly-formatted value (a card number, phone number, or comparable identifier) solely because the user included conventional formatting characters (spaces, dashes, parentheses) the system could trivially strip or normalize instead of forcing the user to retype it in one exact, undocumented format. Distinct from `usability`'s F04, which addresses which keyboard/input mode or autocomplete behavior is presented, not whether an already-entered, validly-formatted value is rejected outright. | Strong | Major | Steve Krug, *Don't Make Me Think, Revisited* (rigid input-formatting rejection as a goodwill depletor) |
| GW03 | Support and error-recovery content actively restores goodwill rather than merely avoiding depletion: help/FAQ content gives genuine, candid answers to the specific questions a user in that context would actually ask, rather than substituting marketing messaging for a real answer (a "Questions We Wish People Would Ask" pattern); an error state names a specific, actionable next step rather than resolving with no path forward; and a step-saving shortcut embedded directly in routine communication (for example a direct order-tracking link inside a confirmation email, rather than requiring login-and-navigate) is treated as a positive goodwill-restoring finding. | Strong | Moderate | Steve Krug, *Don't Make Me Think, Revisited* (goodwill refills: candid FAQs, actionable error recovery, step-saving shortcuts) |

### Folk-rule guard: "always confirm delete"

Do not treat every destructive action as requiring a confirmation dialog. The corrected rule (the fuller routine-vs-irreversible action pattern, A04R-A06R, is covered by the separate `usability` skill): routine, reversible destructive actions should prefer **Undo** over a confirmation dialog — repeated confirmations create habituation and users click through them without reading. Reserve a specific confirmation (naming the action and its consequence, not a generic "Are you sure? Yes/No") for actions that are unusual, irreversible, expensive, or security-sensitive. E05 is precisely about that confirmation being specific and visible; E07 is about not needing a confirmation at all when undo/recovery is available instead.

## How the trust skill should apply these

Check destructive actions first: is the action routine and reversible (favor undo, per A04R-A06R in usability) or irreversible/high-cost (require a specific, consequence-naming confirmation per E05, and never let cancel/back silently discard work per E07)? Then check onboarding and identity requests: flag standalone onboarding unless it teaches something that cannot be learned by using the product (O01-O03), and flag any registration/login step that asks for more than the minimum or fails to explain its benefit (O11), especially when guest/anonymous use would satisfy the same task (O07). When the form collects gender, sex, or other identity/demographic data specifically, also check O13 — separate fields, optional/free-text where possible, and an explicit stated purpose — distinct from O07's broader "is this data needed at all" question. Treat permission-request *timing* as a separate concern handled in `mobile.md` (O04, O05, O08-O10) — this file covers what happens after a permission is denied (O06) and what data is retained afterward (O12). When context about whether identity or a permission is truly required is missing, report `NOT ASSESSABLE` rather than assuming the product needs it.

Apply E08 narrowly and in both directions. It only ever applies to actions that are genuinely high-stakes or security-sensitive (a privacy/security scan, a large financial transfer, an irreversible account-level change) — do not cite it to justify friction on a routine or frequent action, and do not let its absence on a low-stakes action read as a defect. Conversely, an unexplained or clearly artificial delay on a routine action is a finding under E08, not a pass: the rule cuts both ways, so check for missing justified friction on high-stakes actions and unjustified friction on everything else.

When the evidence shows a free trial or subscription signup, check O14: no payment method required upfront, clear pre-conversion notice, and self-service cancellation. This is distinct from `psychology`'s PT04, which governs cancellation-vs-signup friction parity in general — O14 is specifically about *when* payment details are collected for a trial. When the evidence shows a mobile web page, check O15 (no full-screen app-install interstitial blocking content) alongside the onboarding rules. When the evidence shows any priced flow (checkout, subscription, service enrollment), check CT01: are mandatory fees disclosed at the earliest point they can be known, rather than surfaced for the first time at final commitment? Report CT01 as `NOT ASSESSABLE` when the evidence does not show the full flow from entry to final cost.

When the evidence shows a feature that can publish a user's activity to a different or broader audience than where it originated, check O16: is consent an explicit, active, persistent opt-in rather than a passive/auto-expiring opt-out? When the evidence shows a messaging, posting, or reply UI, check O17: is the public-vs-private destination unambiguous right next to the send control, not just configurable elsewhere? When the evidence shows a cloud-sync or storage toggle with a warning dialog, check O18: does the warning state where files actually live and what happens to any local copy, rather than using vague removal language?

Apply the Reservoir of Goodwill model (GW01) across the whole reviewed evidence, not as a single-screen check: look for the taxonomy of depletors (hidden information, disproportionate data requests, hollow sincerity copy, task-blocking promotion, amateurish execution) cumulatively, since the model's point is that friction compounds rather than each instance standing alone. When the evidence shows a form field accepting a card number, phone number, or comparable identifier, check GW02: does the system normalize conventional formatting characters instead of rejecting the input outright? This is independent of GW01 — it is a validation-logic defect, not an information-withholding or tone defect. When the evidence shows support/FAQ content, an error-recovery state, or a transactional communication (a confirmation email, a receipt), check GW03: are FAQ answers candid rather than marketing-dressed, does an error state name a specific next step, and does routine communication embed available step-saving shortcuts?

When the evidence shows a transaction, booking, or submission flow, check PR01: does the flow account for a plausible gap between on-screen completion and the user's goal actually being realized (a confirmation email, a status update during a wait), or does it stop at the completion screen and leave that gap unaddressed? Do not apply PR01 when the transaction completes fully and immediately with nothing left to wait for. When the evidence shows a background protective or maintenance system operating silently (a security scan, an auto-backup, a threat/ad blocker), check PR02: does it surface a periodic, non-intrusive activity summary, or does its total silence risk the user concluding the protection was unnecessary and disabling it?

When the evidence shows a free trial screen, check O19 alongside O14: are the duration, what ends with the trial, and the later charge stated before the trial starts? When the evidence shows a struck-through or "was" price, check CT02: is the former price one the item was really sold at? Report CT02 as `NOT ASSESSABLE` when the evidence gives no price history.

When the evidence shows a product page, check CT03 beside CT01: is an estimated shipping cost shown, and is the return policy linked? Report CT03 as `NOT ASSESSABLE` when the evidence does not show the buy section.

Baymard Institute research rules added in this file: CT04, CT05, CT06, CT07, CT08. Check each when the evidence shows its surface (forms and checkout, navigation, search and filters, accounts and orders, delivery and returns); each reports `NOT ASSESSABLE` for parts the evidence does not show.
