# Product: goal, value, content

Platform-agnostic. Covers rules P01-P10 (purpose & task) and C01-C30 (content) from the source catalog. Information-architecture rules (IA01-IA09) live in `information-architecture.md`, not here. See `../shared/evidence-model.md` and `severity-model.md` for how to report findings from these rules.

## Purpose & task

| ID | Rule | Evidence | Default severity | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| P01 | The screen has a clear primary user goal; content and controls support that goal rather than internal product structure. | Strong | Major | Apple HIG Design principles; NN/g Ten Usability Heuristics; ISO 9241-210 |
| P02 | The most important information/action appears before secondary information; visual hierarchy reflects task priority. | Strong | Major | Apple HIG Writing; Android content composition guidance; NN/g Ten Usability Heuristics; Nielsen Norman Group, Schade, "The Fold Manifesto: Why the Page Fold Still Matters" (2015) |
| P03 | The design minimizes unnecessary steps, decisions, data entry, and mode switches for the main task. | Strong | Major | Apple HIG Design principles; NN/g Ten Usability Heuristics, Progressive Disclosure; Baymard mcommerce research |
| P04 | Common tasks are efficient; advanced/rare options are progressively disclosed rather than competing with the primary path. | Strong | Moderate | NN/g Progressive Disclosure; Apple HIG Design principles |
| P05 | The user can leave or cancel guided flows when lock-in is not essential. | Strong | Major | Apple HIG Design principles, Onboarding; NN/g Ten Usability Heuristics |
| P06 | The app does not force registration before demonstrating value unless identity is intrinsic to the task or required for safety/security. | Contextual | Major | NN/g Checklist for Registration/Login, Login Walls Stop Users; ISO 9241-210 |
| P07 | The design distinguishes first-use, repeat-use, and expert-use needs; it does not optimize exclusively for first-time explanation. | Strong | Moderate | NN/g Progressive Disclosure, Mobile-App Onboarding, Mobile UX Study Guide |
| P08 | A feature, permission request, or onboarding flow states a concrete user value, why it is the best available way to serve that value, and explicit operating boundaries for when/how it is used; a vague, open-ended value proposition ("stay connected," "never miss out") does not justify unconstrained, unbounded access. | Contextual | Major | Stanford Behavior Design Lab, Fogg Behavior Model; Apple HIG Privacy, Requesting Permission; Android Request runtime permissions |
| P09 | On a product-detail page built around one featured item, cross-sell/recommendation modules ("Similar products", "Customers who bought this") are positioned below or beside the primary product's details, not above them; placing them above the featured product delays and reduces attention paid to the primary product's own images, specs, and reviews. | Strong | Moderate | Romano Bergstrom & Schall (eds.), *Eye Tracking in User Experience Design* (2014) |
| P10 | A semi-autonomous feature (an AI co-pilot, an autopilot-style automation) that automates most of a task while expecting a human to maintain continuous passive vigilance for a rare failure, and hands control back only at the moment of that failure with no advance lead time, is a design defect — not a training or attention problem. The feature needs either full automation of the task, or an active-engagement design with explicit, periodic decision checkpoints that keep the human meaningfully involved. Distinct from `usability`'s IE14 (background automation must not seize focus/interrupt active input — an interruption concern during active use, not vigilance decay during passive monitoring). | Strong | Major | Don Norman, *Design for a Better World* (semi-automation complacency trap; self-driving-car complacency research; Intelligence Amplification framing) |
| P11 | Instructional or coaching content for a feature is delivered in-context at the moment a user attempts or needs that specific feature (a short contextual tip, an inline demonstration), rather than front-loaded as a mandatory upfront tour, walkthrough, or manual read before any real task need exists. Distinct from P07, which establishes that first-use, repeat-use, and expert-use needs differ, without specifying *when* instructional content should be delivered — P11 addresses delivery timing specifically. Comprehensive reference documentation available on request, not forced on the user before any task, does not violate this rule. | Strong | Major | Don Norman, *Living with Complexity* (just-in-time contextual learning vs. upfront manuals/tours; Kodak EasyShare's brief in-store/on-device contextual demonstrations) |

**Applicability note:** P06 is contextual, not a hard fail — some products legitimately require identity before any value can be shown (e.g. a service tied to a personal account by nature). Ask what the product actually is before flagging.

**Applicability note:** P08 is distinct from P06 — P06 covers only identity/personal-data gating, while P08 covers any feature or permission whose stated benefit is too vague to bound its use, whether or not identity/data is involved. When the evidence does not show whether an alternative approach was considered or why this approach best serves the stated value, report P08 as `NOT ASSESSABLE` rather than assuming the flow passes or fails.

**Applicability note:** P09 applies only to a product-detail page organized around one featured item (a single product, listing, or article page with cross-sell/recommendation modules attached). It does not apply to browse, category, or listing pages where recommendation or "you might also like" modules are themselves the primary content — on those pages, judge hierarchy under the general P02 rule instead. P09 is a specific, citable instance of P02's general hierarchy concern for this one common layout, not a replacement for it.

## Content / UX writing

| ID | Rule | Evidence | Default severity | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C01 | Copy is concise, direct, respectful, and uses the user's vocabulary rather than internal implementation terms. | Strong | Moderate | Apple HIG Writing; NN/g Ten Usability Heuristics, Error-Message Guidelines |
| C02 | Buttons and links use descriptive action language; avoid cute wording when clarity matters. | Strong | Moderate | Apple HIG Writing, Buttons; Nielsen Norman Group, Sherwin, "'Learn More' Links: You Can Do Better" (2015); Nielsen Norman Group, Pernice, "A Link is a Promise" (2014) |
| C03 | Multi-step flows use consistent labels for progress actions such as Next/Continue/Done and clearly indicate completion. | Strong | Moderate | Apple HIG Writing |
| C04 | Empty/error states explain what happened and what to do next, rather than merely decorating the blank state. | Strong | Major | NN/g Designing Empty States, Error-Message Guidelines; Apple HIG Writing; Don Norman, *Living with Complexity* ("the natural world has no error messages" — explain and guide rather than scold) |
| C05 | Inclusive language avoids unnecessary assumptions, stereotypes, and culture-specific ambiguity; localization implications are reviewed. Translation and cultural connotation are the least predictable layer across territories (Giles Colborne's hierarchy of localization predictability: core task flows are highly consistent across markets, infrastructure/legal/payment norms vary but predictably, while language and cultural symbolism vary the most and require dedicated local review). | Strong | Moderate | Apple HIG Inclusion; Android Localize your app; Jesmond Allen & James Chudley, *Smashing UX Design* (Giles Colborne's localization-predictability hierarchy) |
| C06 | Critical instructions are placed where needed in context rather than in a distant help screen that requires recall. | Strong | Major | NN/g Ten Usability Heuristics, Progressive Disclosure |
| C13 | An opt-out or decline control (for example dismissing a promotional modal or lead-generation popup) does not force the user to select a derogatory, self-deprecating, or guilt-inducing statement in order to decline; decline language stays neutral ("No thanks", "Close"). | Strong | Moderate | NN/g Deceptive Patterns in UX |
| C14 | When exact technical precision would make copy unreadably long for its container, intermediate positions on a concision-precision spectrum are tested rather than defaulting to either extreme; if no position is both accurate and readable, the underlying system logic being described — not the copy — is the root finding. | Contextual | Moderate | Apple HIG Writing; NN/g Ten Usability Heuristics (extends this file's own C01 concision guidance) |
| C15 | Language describing disability or human traits avoids tragic ("confined to a wheelchair") or patronizing/heroic ("bravely overcame") framing in favor of neutral, objective phrasing ("uses a wheelchair"). | Strong | Moderate | Apple HIG Inclusion (extends this file's own C05 inclusive-language guidance) |
| C16 | Product copy referring to an unspecified third party (for example a future support agent) defaults to singular "they"/"them"/"their" rather than "(s)he" or "he/she" constructions. | Strong | Minor | Apple HIG Inclusion; Android Localize your app (extends this file's own C05 inclusive-language guidance) |
| C17 | Opt-in/opt-out checkbox and form logic does not use a double negative or inverted logic (for example a checkbox that must be checked to decline an offer) that forces the user to work out what checking or unchecking the control actually does. Distinct from C13 (confirm-shaming, which addresses emotionally loaded language): C17 addresses logical inversion, whether or not the phrasing is emotionally loaded. | Strong | Major | Falbe, Frederiksen & Andersen, *White Hat UX* (Trick Questions pattern) |
| C18 | Help/FAQ content is structured around actual user task goals and empirically validated frequent inquiries (for example support-log analytics), not arbitrary questions invented by internal staff to fill out a format. | Strong | Moderate | Jesse James Garrett, *The Elements of User Experience* (format-vs-purpose FAQ trap) |
| C19 | Photography that informs a user's decision — demonstrating scale, quality, craftsmanship, or directly addressing a stated concern — is used in place of purely decorative, generic stock photography that conveys no product or service information. | Strong | Moderate | Jesmond Allen & James Chudley, *Smashing UX Design* (content photography vs. ornamental stock photography) |
| C20 | A data table, chart, or dashboard is prioritized and filtered for the actual target audience's expertise level, not the data owner's or a domain expert's — the "expert knowledge trap." Non-essential columns, dense figures, and unexplained jargon are stripped from the primary view for a general/novice audience rather than exposing every parameter a specialist would want. | Contextual | Moderate | Jesmond Allen & James Chudley, *Smashing UX Design* (expert knowledge trap in data visualization); NN/g Progressive Disclosure; Marvin Minsky, ingredients-of-intelligence framing — subgoals, sub-objects, cause-symbols (via Sean Adams, *How Design Makes Us Think and Feel and Do Things*) |
| C21 | Content whose credibility a user must judge — an article, editorial content, a published report, or comparable long-form material — displays four validity signals: source of information, references/citations to trustworthy material, a visible creation/publication date, and author attribution demonstrating relevant expertise. Distinct from C01-C06 (content clarity/wording) and `references/voice-tone.md` (tone): C21 is about credibility signaling, not phrasing. | Strong | Moderate | Mark Wells, *User Experience Design* (content validity and authenticity indicators) |
| C22 | A chart, graph, or dashboard's visualization type matches its underlying data category — quantities (bar/pie/bubble/heat map), locations (scale or chorochromatic maps), or connections (tree diagrams, mind maps, flow charts) — and follows basic graphical-perception conventions: simple, relevant, clearly defined, visually salient, and consistent with standard chart-reading conventions. Distinct from C20, which governs whether displayed *complexity* fits the audience's expertise, not whether the chosen chart *type* fits the data; a dashboard can pass C20 (rightly scoped for its audience) while still failing C22 (wrong chart type for the data), or vice versa. | Strong | Moderate | Stephen Kosslyn, graphical-perception rules; Per Mollerup, quantities/locations/connections taxonomy (via Mark Wells, *User Experience Design*); Marvin Minsky, ingredients-of-intelligence framing — subgoals, sub-objects, cause-symbols (via Sean Adams, *How Design Makes Us Think and Feel and Do Things*) |
| C23 | The level of visual production polish (photographic quality, animation, decorative styling) matches the trust expectations of the verified target audience, rather than assuming maximum polish is always correct. Excessive glossiness that a specific audience reads as inflated cost, inauthenticity, or a mismatch with the product's actual value proposition (for example a professional trade/wholesale audience) is flagged as a trust-undermining mismatch, not praised as a positive finding. | Contextual | Moderate | Daniel Miller, "trapping the audience" research (via Mark Wells, *User Experience Design*); deliberately raw/unpolished visual production as a signal of urgency or grassroots authenticity in crisis/activism contexts, the inverse case (via Sean Adams, *How Design Makes Us Think and Feel and Do Things*) |
| C24 | A primary action button (a checkout, upgrade, or final submission button) states its concrete, dynamic outcome — substituting a real computed value (e.g. "Place Order for $24.57") — rather than only a generic verb ("Submit", "Continue", "OK"), whenever that outcome is already known at the point the button is shown. Distinct from C02 (descriptive action language in general, which does not require real-time value substitution). | Strong | Moderate | Anderson, *Seductive Interaction Design* (dynamic outcome-explicit button copy) |
| C25 | A dashboard, health score, or other composite metric does not blend multiple, especially trade-off, dimensions (e.g. cost and quality, or growth and risk) into a single number without drill-down access to the constituent categories. Distinct from C20 (audience-appropriate complexity, not aggregation) and C22 (chart-type fit, not whether an aggregate hides sub-category failures): a blended composite can mask a failing sub-category behind an acceptable-looking aggregate. | Strong | Major | Don Norman, *Design for a Better World* (Single Aggregate Number Fallacy; Kate Raworth's Doughnut Model; the GDP critique — pollution and its cleanup cost both counted as positive economic activity); Marvin Minsky, ingredients-of-intelligence framing — subgoals, sub-objects, cause-symbols (via Sean Adams, *How Design Makes Us Think and Feel and Do Things*) |
| C26 | A data-dense screen centered on a specific human subject or record (a patient, a customer, an individual transaction) keeps a visible human/narrative context anchor — a name, a short summary, relevant history — alongside clinical, numeric, or system-status fields, rather than presenting isolated numbers with no human context. | Strong | Moderate | Don Norman, *Design for a Better World* ("Medical Cart Syndrome" — hospital rounds conducted via computer cart readouts rather than the patient); Marvin Minsky, ingredients-of-intelligence framing — subgoals, sub-objects, cause-symbols (via Sean Adams, *How Design Makes Us Think and Feel and Do Things*) |
| C28 | A medical, legal, grave, or trauma-related screen uses an objective, restrained visual/photographic tone — no cheerful stock imagery or patronizing decoration — matched to the content's gravity. The visual counterpart to C15's language-level neutrality rule: C15 governs wording, C28 governs imagery and decorative styling. | Contextual | Major | Atelier Brückner, *Death and the Afterlife* exhibition design; Michael Bierut, *The Babysitter* poster (via Sean Adams, *How Design Makes Us Think and Feel and Do Things*) |
| C29 | A product designed for children, teens, or older adults does not use age-patronizing visual tropes — fake handwriting-style fonts, chaotic decorative color with no functional purpose, or artificial dumbing-down — that assume reduced intelligence, in place of clean, bold, high-legibility design that respects the audience's actual competence. Parallel to C15's disability-dignity rule but keyed to age rather than disability; does not prohibit design genuinely adapted to real motor-skill, literacy, or visual-acuity needs. | Contextual | Moderate | Sean Adams, Nickelodeon "Kid Modernism" rebrand (*How Design Makes Us Think and Feel and Do Things*) |
| C30 | A platform hosting user-generated or curated content (video, portfolios, documents, or comparable media) keeps its own branding minimal and non-competing — no animated logos, aggressive watermarks, or persistent banners overlaid on the content itself. | Strong | Moderate | Sean Adams & Samantha Jan Fleming, Sundance Channel non-interfering "Bob" broadcast identity system (via Sean Adams, *How Design Makes Us Think and Feel and Do Things*) |
| C31 | A tagline positioned adjacent to a site or product's identity/logo states a concrete, differentiating value proposition in roughly 6-8 words, rather than a vague corporate motto ("Here with you, for you", "World-class solutions") that conveys no functional information a first-time visitor could act on. A globally recognized brand whose utility is already common public knowledge is exempt from stating a functional value proposition and may use a purely aspirational or brand-affinity tagline instead. | Strong | Moderate | Steve Krug, *Don't Make Me Think, Revisited* (tagline excellence criteria) |
| C32 | A performance metric on a dashboard or summary appears with at least one comparison that lets the viewer judge it — a target, a prior period, or a variance — and not as a bare number. Distinct from C22 (chart type) and C25 (blended composites). Does not apply to secondary values whose context is evident. | Expert practice | Moderate | Stephen Few, *Information Dashboard Design: The Effective Visual Communication of Data* (O'Reilly, 2006) — author's stated practice; no study cited |
| C33 | Numbers on a summary display use the precision the decision needs (for example "$3.8M" in an executive overview) and not full precision. Does not apply where exact values are the task (accounting, audit, scientific readings). Distinct from C14, which concerns precision of copy. | Expert practice | Minor | Stephen Few, *Information Dashboard Design: The Effective Visual Communication of Data* (O'Reilly, 2006) — author's stated practice; no study cited |
| C34 | A table is used for looking up exact values and a graph for perceiving shape, trend, or exceptions; a radial gauge, a pseudo-3D chart, or a radar chart is replaced by a linear or bar form unless the category axis is naturally cyclic (hours of a day). Distinct from C22, which matches chart type to data category. Does not apply to a decorative illustration that carries no data; a display the evidence does not show is `NOT ASSESSABLE`. | Expert practice | Moderate | Stephen Few, *Information Dashboard Design: The Effective Visual Communication of Data* (O'Reilly, 2006) — author's stated practice; no study cited |
| C35 | A product page shows a descriptive product name, recognisable and enlargeable images, a price that states additional charges, product options with their availability, an add-to-cart control that confirms the action, and a concise description. Distinct from C19, which concerns whether photography informs a decision. Does not apply to a page that is not a purchase page; elements the evidence does not show are `NOT ASSESSABLE`. | Strong | Major | Nielsen Norman Group, Sherwin, "UX Guidelines for Ecommerce Product Pages" (2019); Nielsen Norman Group, Wang, "Does Your Form Really Need a Dropdown List?" **Applicability note:** C23 requires a verified target audience in evidence before it can be scored; when the evidence does not establish who the specific audience is or what they expect, report C23 as `NOT ASSESSABLE` rather than assuming a consumer or specialist audience.

**Applicability note:** C20 does not apply when the evidence confirms the actual audience is domain specialists using a professional/expert tool — the same verified-expert-audience exception C01 already states below applies here: high information density for a confirmed expert audience is not itself a defect.

**Applicability note:** C01's plain-language expectation does not apply when the verified target audience is domain specialists using a professional/expert tool (for example an engineering component-sizing calculator); established domain terminology ("torque", "viscosity") is correct there, not a violation, and should not be flagged for lacking a consumer-friendly explanation.

**Applicability note:** C01's user-vocabulary expectation extends beyond navigation labels (which `usability`'s N07R already covers) to every action label/button across the product: the same action should carry one consistent label everywhere it appears, rather than introducing synonyms ("Delete" in one screen, "Erase" or "Remove" for the identical action elsewhere) that force the user to recall whether the two words mean the same thing instead of recognizing the action instantly.

## How the product skill should apply these

1. Start every review by identifying the single primary user goal for the reviewed screen/flow (P01). If none is identifiable, that is itself a P01 finding, not a gap to skip past.
2. Check P02 (hierarchy) and C01-C06 plus C13-C16 (content) against that same stated goal — content quality is judged by whether it serves the goal, not by taste. If the reviewed screen is a product-detail page built around one featured item, also check P09: cross-sell/recommendation modules must not sit above the primary product's own details. Do not apply P09 to browse/category/listing pages, where recommendation modules can legitimately be the primary content — judge those under P02 alone. Once content is checked, also check the copy's voice and tone against `references/voice-tone.md`'s VT01-VT06 — this is a distinct evaluative axis from content clarity, not a restatement of it. When the evidence includes photography, check C19: does it inform the user's decision, or is it purely decorative stock imagery in a spot that would benefit from real content photography? When the evidence includes a data table, chart, or dashboard, check C20 (audience-appropriate complexity) and C22 (chart-type-fit) together — a dashboard can independently pass or fail each. When the evidence is content-heavy material whose credibility matters to the user (an article, editorial content, a published report), check C21: source, citations, publication date, and author attribution. When the evidence includes photography, video, animation, or other production-value choices, check C23: does the level of polish match what the verified target audience actually trusts, rather than assuming more polish is always better? Report C23 as `NOT ASSESSABLE` when the target audience is not established. When the evidence includes a primary checkout, upgrade, or final submission button and its concrete outcome (e.g. an order total) is already known at that point in the flow, check C24: does the button state that outcome directly, rather than only a generic verb?
