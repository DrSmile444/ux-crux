# Visual hierarchy & consistency

Platform-agnostic. Covers rules VH01-VH10. See `../shared/evidence-model.md` and `severity-model.md` for how to report findings from these rules.

This is the "Surface plane" of a reviewed product: the deliberate use of visual contrast to signal meaning, and the grid-based consistency that holds a multi-screen or multi-team product together as one coherent system. It is distinct from the Actions/Forms/Navigation sections of `core.md` (which govern what controls exist and how they behave) and from `product`'s content/voice-tone rules (which govern wording) — this file governs what the eye perceives before any wording or interaction is evaluated.

| ID | Rule | Evidence | Default severity | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| VH01 | Visual differences between adjacent elements (color, weight, size) are bold and deliberate when they signal a real functional difference; near-identical-but-not-quite-identical styling between elements that should either clearly match or clearly differ creates cognitive friction ("is this a mistake?"). Elements that are functionally identical are styled identically. Digital interfaces strip away vocal tone and body language, the two channels research (Rushkoff; Koneya & Barbour's 7-38-55 finding) attributes the large majority of human communicated meaning to — which is why visual/spatial layout itself must carry the operational meaning this rule protects, rather than relying on text alone to compensate. | Strong | Moderate | Jesse James Garrett, *The Elements of User Experience* (intentional contrast vs. confusing near-uniformity); Douglas Rushkoff; Koneya & Barbour, non-verbal communication research (via Mark Wells, *User Experience Design*) |
| VH02 | Layouts use a shared grid system for internal consistency (a central style guide prevents fragmented styles across sub-sections/teams) and external consistency (brand credibility across the whole product); the grid is revisited and updated when it no longer accommodates new functionality, rather than being treated as an untouchable constraint that forces new content into an outdated layout. | Strong | Moderate | Jesse James Garrett, *The Elements of User Experience* (grid-based layouts, internal/external consistency) |
| VH03 | A purely decorative visual or motion element (a gratuitous intro animation, ambient background motion, an auto-playing video banner, or an ornamental graphic with no functional/informational purpose) is minimized or removed when it measurably competes with legibility, contrast, or attention on task-critical content ("flash and trash"). Distinct from VH01, which addresses whether meaningful elements use deliberate contrast, not whether decoration itself is present. A short-term promotional/marketing landing page, where initial visual engagement is itself the goal, is weighed more lightly under this rule. | Strong | Moderate | Hartson & Pyla, *The UX Book* ("flash and trash" / gratuitous graphics); Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* (1983), data-ink and chartjunk (via Few, *Information Dashboard Design*, 2006) |
| VH04 | A heading sits visually closer to the section it introduces than to the section that precedes it — the space above a heading is greater than the space below it, not equal to or less than it. A heading that "floats" equidistant between (or closer to) the preceding section breaks the visual-grouping cue a scanning user relies on to know which content the heading actually belongs to. | Strong | Moderate | Steve Krug, *Don't Make Me Think, Revisited* (one of four rules Krug states as an unconditional "never") |
| VH05 | A page does not give the impression of ending before its content does: a full-screen hero image or video is followed by a visible sliver of the next section or another cue that invites scrolling, and full-width rules, wide empty gaps, fade-in sections and large ad or promo blocks do not sit where the user would read them as the end of the page. NN/g observed most test users in one study never scrolling past a full-screen hero. Distinct from PA12, which concerns auto-rotating carousels that hide content, D10, which chooses between infinite scroll and Load more, and L11, which concerns ad placement on mobile pages. Does not apply to a page that is genuinely one screen long and to a single-purpose search or sign-in screen; where the fold falls on the user's device and what lies below the captured area is `NOT ASSESSABLE`. | Strong | Moderate | Nielsen Norman Group, Flaherty, "The Illusion of Completeness: What It Is and How to Avoid It" (2016); Nielsen Norman Group, Wang, "Homepage Design: 5 Fundamental Principles" (2024); Nielsen Norman Group, Fessenden, "Scrolling and Attention" (2018); Nielsen Norman Group, Paul, "Scroll Fading 101" (2023) |
| VH06 | Content that continues sideways (a carousel, filmstrip, swipe deck or wide table) shows a persistent cue inside the content area that more exists: a partly visible next item, arrow controls visible without hover, and a position or count indicator; small or faint dots alone and hover-only arrows do not count, an arrow, dot or other control placed over an image keeps enough contrast to be seen, and dots sit centred directly below the content they control and not on a busy image. Page-control dots or a swipe are not the only way to reach key content or features (a link, tab or list also leads there), and on desktop essential content is not reachable only by sideways scrolling or swiping. Distinct from PA12 and PA20, which concern reliance on carousels and their auto-rotation, C37 and C45, which concern colour-swatch rows and product-image thumbnails, and T10, which concerns how many swipes a mobile carousel needs. Does not apply to short rows that fit fully in view and to decorative galleries whose images carry no task content; whether users reach the later items in real use and contrast under real glare is `NOT ASSESSABLE`. | Contextual | Moderate | Nielsen Norman Group, Sherwin, "Beware Horizontal Scrolling and Mimicking Swipe on Desktop" (2014); Nielsen Norman Group, Flaherty, "The Illusion of Completeness: What It Is and How to Avoid It" (2016); Nielsen Norman Group, Schade, Harley, Pernice and Budiu, "4 iOS Rules to Break" (2015); Nielsen Norman Group, Budiu, "Carousels on Mobile Devices" (2018); Nielsen Norman Group, Schade, "Mobile Tables: Comparisons and Other Data Tables" (2017) |
| VH07 | A large hero image or video does not push the page's primary task entry (search, booking, the main call to action) below the first screen or reduce it to a low-contrast outline on the image; the primary task keeps at least the visual weight of the image. NN/g contrasted a redesign that hid flight search below a landscape image with the earlier layout and with a mobile page whose actions matched the image in emphasis. Distinct from A01R, which concerns a clear primary action on each screen in general, and from PA04, which concerns deliberate emphasis, not an image dominating the first screen. Does not apply to pages whose single task is browsing images, or to search-only pages where the search box already stands out; how the page renders on other viewports is `NOT ASSESSABLE`. | Contextual | Moderate | Nielsen Norman Group, Whitenton, "Image-Focused Design: Is Bigger Better?" (2014); Nielsen Norman Group, Wang, "Homepage Design: 5 Fundamental Principles" (2024) |
| VH08 | On content pages, subheadings are visually different from body text in a consistent way (size, weight, colour or typeface) without looking like an ad, each subheading describes all of its section and only its section, and in card or grid layouts each chunk has a label that sits clearly closer to its own content so users can tell which label belongs to which content. Distinct from VH04, which requires the space above a heading to exceed the space below it, and does not check how a heading is styled or whether its wording matches its section. Does not apply to pages with very little text and single-block messages; users' actual eye movements is `NOT ASSESSABLE`. | Strong | Moderate | Nielsen Norman Group, Kara Pernice, "The Layer-Cake Pattern of Scanning Content on the Web" (2019) |
| VH09 | When a list marks items that differ in one attribute (new and on sale, up and down, open and closed), each indicator type differs from the others in both an icon or shape and a colour, not in its text label alone; where only one cue fits, an icon with a clear meaning is preferred to colour alone. Distinct from `X02`, which requires a non-colour cue so that colour is not the only channel, not a cue that speeds up finding marked items in a list. Does not apply to a list that uses one indicator type only; how fast users find marked items on the live page is `NOT ASSESSABLE`. | Strong | Moderate | Nielsen Norman Group, Harley, "Visual Indicators to Differentiate Items in a List" (2016) |
| VH10 | Decorative images in a repeated image-and-text list sit on the same side down the page; alternating image sides (a zigzag) is used only where the images carry information users need or the list has only a few rows; images that contain text or dense detail are not placed where they compete with the text beside them. Distinct from `VH03`, which concerns removing decoration that competes with content, not where kept images sit in a list. Does not apply to informative images such as product photos and explanatory diagrams; how users scan the live page is `NOT ASSESSABLE`. | Strong | Minor | Nielsen Norman Group, Flaherty, "Zigzag Image–Text Layouts Make Scanning Less Efficient" (2017) |

**Applicability note:** VH01 requires a stated or reasonably inferable functional difference (or lack of one) between the compared elements. If the evidence does not show whether two similarly-styled elements are meant to be the same or different, report `NOT ASSESSABLE` rather than assuming an inconsistency.

**Applicability note:** VH02's "outdated grid" scenario requires evidence that the current grid actually fails to accommodate needed content or functionality (e.g. visible cramping, overflow, or ad hoc exceptions to the grid). A grid that is merely old, with no demonstrated failure to fit current content, is not itself a finding.

### The Squint test (technique, not a separate rule)

To evaluate whether a layout's visual hierarchy matches its task priority (`product`'s P02) and whether contrast is used deliberately (VH01), blur or squint at the layout and check two things: (1) which element the eye lands on first, and (2) whether the eye is then guided through the remaining options in priority order. If a decorative or secondary element dominates the blurred view while the primary call-to-action recedes into the background, that is the same P02/VH01 finding stated concretely — this technique is a way to surface and demonstrate that finding, not a rule competing with P02 or VH01.

## How the usability skill should apply these

Check VH01 and VH02 whenever the evidence includes a visual layout (a screenshot, a design comp, or a description of one) rather than only a textual flow description. Apply the Squint test above alongside `product`'s P02 to ground any visual-hierarchy finding in a concrete, repeatable check rather than a subjective taste call. When multiple screens or sections of the same product are in evidence, compare their grids and styling for VH02's internal-consistency scenario before judging any single screen in isolation. When the evidence shows motion, animation, or decorative graphics — especially on a task-critical or status-monitoring surface rather than a short-term marketing page — also check VH03 for whether the decoration measurably reduces legibility or competes with task-critical attention. When the evidence shows body content with headings (an article, a settings screen with sub-sections, a long form), also check VH04: does each heading sit visibly closer to its own section than to the one above it, or does it read as floating between the two?

Nielsen Norman Group research rules added in this file: VH05, VH06, VH07, VH08, VH09, VH10. Check each when the evidence shows its surface; each reports `NOT ASSESSABLE` for parts the evidence does not show.
