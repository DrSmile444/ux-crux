# Product: text-scanning and typography for content-heavy pages

Applies to text-heavy or web-rendered content (articles, landing pages, long-form informational pages). See `../../shared/evidence-model.md` and `severity-model.md` for how to report findings from these rules. These rules extend `core.md`'s Content / UX writing section (C01-C06) and continue its ID series; they apply in addition to, not instead of, `core.md`'s content rules.

| ID | Rule | Evidence | Default severity | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C07 | Structure copy in an inverted-pyramid order: lead with the core conclusion/takeaway in the first sentence, present supporting detail in descending order of importance, and reserve background/context for the end. | Strong | Moderate | Romano Bergstrom & Schall (eds.), *Eye Tracking in User Experience Design* (2014); WCAG 2.2 Visual Presentation (SC 1.4.8, Level AAA: text not justified) |
| C08 | Place high-information, intent-matching keywords within the first 3-4 words of headlines, subheadings, and bullet points; avoid opening with filler or passive framing that delays the meaningful word. | Strong | Moderate | Romano Bergstrom & Schall (eds.), *Eye Tracking in User Experience Design* (2014); Nielsen Norman Group, Nielsen, "First 2 Words: A Signal for the Scanning Eye" (2009); Nielsen Norman Group, Loranger, "Headings Are Pick-Up Lines: 5 Tips for Writing Headlines That Convert" (2015) |
| C09 | Avoid ALL-CAPS for headlines, body copy, and button labels; use sentence case. ALL-CAPS text destroys word-shape recognition (ascenders/descenders) and measurably slows reading. | Strong | Moderate | Romano Bergstrom & Schall (eds.), *Eye Tracking in User Experience Design* (2014) |
| C10 | Keep body paragraphs left-aligned; avoid centering or fully justifying multi-line text. A jagged left edge or justification-driven uneven word spacing forces extra effort to locate the start of each line. | Strong | Moderate | Romano Bergstrom & Schall (eds.), *Eye Tracking in User Experience Design* (2014) |
| C11 | Write numbers as digits (e.g. "23") rather than spelling them out as words, including at the start of a sentence or bullet point; digits form a distinct visual shape that draws fixations during scanning. | Strong | Minor | Romano Bergstrom & Schall (eds.), *Eye Tracking in User Experience Design* (2014) |
| C12 | Pair important images with a short, information-rich caption placed directly beneath or adjacent to the image; captions are among the most consistently fixated and remembered text elements on a page. | Strong | Moderate | Romano Bergstrom & Schall (eds.), *Eye Tracking in User Experience Design* (2014) |
| C27 | Long-form or editorial body text renders at roughly 50-70 characters per line, with heading sizes proportionally larger than body text rather than an arbitrary or negligible scale difference. | Strong | Moderate | Classical book-page proportions (via Sean Adams, *How Design Makes Us Think and Feel and Do Things*); NN/g line-length readability guidance; WCAG 2.2 Visual Presentation (SC 1.4.8, Level AAA: line width up to 80 characters) |
| C97 | Content that people read on screen is published as an HTML page, not only as a PDF; where a PDF is also needed for printing or download, an HTML gateway page summarises the key points and links to the file with its format and size (and page count for a long document), and site search and other links point to that page and not straight to the file; any link that leads to a PDF, a media player or another application names the format. Distinct from X28, which concerns essential text being real HTML text and not an image, and A09R, which concerns a control's effect matching its label. Does not apply to legal, print-only or to-be-signed documents that must exist as PDF, or to short forms the user asked to download; PDF accessibility tagging without the file, and the reading context of a given audience, are `NOT ASSESSABLE`. | Contextual | Moderate | Nielsen Norman Group, Nielsen and Kaley, "Avoid PDF for On-Screen Reading" (2020); Nielsen Norman Group, Nielsen and Kaley, "PDF: Still Unfit for Human Consumption, 20 Years Later" (2020); Nielsen Norman Group, Nielsen, "Gateway Pages Prevent PDF Shock" (2003); Nielsen Norman Group, Nielsen, "113 Design Guidelines for Homepage Usability" (2001); Nielsen Norman Group, Nielsen, "Top 10 Mistakes in Web Design" (2011) |
| C98 | Informational and product copy states plain, checkable facts instead of boastful, subjective promotional claims (hottest ever, world-class, best); a claim is backed by a specific figure, feature or source. In NN/g tests of the same site written in several versions, objective wording improved task performance over promotional wording, and users read promotional wording as less credible. Distinct from C01, which asks for concise, direct copy in the user's vocabulary, VT01, which ranks clarity above cleverness, and GW01, which flags hollow sincerity copy as a goodwill depletor, not the substitution of unsupported superlatives with facts. Does not apply to advertising, campaign, brand or entertainment pages whose purpose is promotion; how a given audience reads the tone, and the effect on conversion, are `NOT ASSESSABLE`. | Strong | Minor | Nielsen Norman Group, Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web" (1997); Nielsen Norman Group, Morkes and Nielsen, "Applying Writing Guidelines to Web Pages" (1998) |
| C99 | The top of a page does not spend prime space on a welcome salutation or generic filler such as a greeting; any introductory text is short and states what the page contains, and a tagline takes the place of a welcome message. Distinct from C31, which concerns a concrete tagline beside the identity area, and C07, which concerns the order of body copy. Does not apply to a page whose first text is task-critical instruction; how many users skip the text is `NOT ASSESSABLE`. | Contextual | Minor | Nielsen Norman Group, Nielsen, "Blah-Blah Text: Keep, Cut, or Kill?" (2007); Nielsen Norman Group, Nielsen, "113 Design Guidelines for Homepage Usability" (2001) |
| C100 | A long-form page or article offers a summary or key-takeaways block near the top, with a descriptive heading (such as Summary or Key Takeaways) and a visual treatment that sets it apart from the body; a recap at the end of a very long piece is not the only summary. NN/g's usability study of long content found that summaries help users decide whether a page is relevant and give them a roadmap. Distinct from C07, which orders the body copy as an inverted pyramid, and C27, which concerns line length and heading scale in long text, not an upfront summary block. Does not apply to short pages or to content whose first paragraph already states the conclusion; whether readers use the summary on the reviewed page is `NOT ASSESSABLE`. | Strong | Minor | Nielsen Norman Group, Wang and Chan, "5 Formatting Techniques for Long-Form Content" (2023) |
| C101 | A long text passage is broken into sections with descriptive subheadings and uses short bulleted lists where the content is list-like; bulleted items are short, or each long item starts with a bolded key phrase, and bold or highlight marks only the few critical fragments, not whole paragraphs. NN/g studies found that readers scan headings and list items, that a participant missed a visible answer inside lengthy unformatted bullets, and that a scannable layout beat the same text as plain paragraphs. Distinct from C08, which concerns the first words of headings and bullets, and PA10, which concerns breaking a dense text column into blocks to spread fixations, not whether a passage has headings and lists or how list items and emphasis are formatted. Does not apply to short passages and text that users commit to read in full, nor, for the bolded lead-in clause, to lists meant to be read in order such as step-by-step instructions; how far readers actually read is `NOT ASSESSABLE`. | Strong | Moderate | Nielsen Norman Group, Pernice, "Text Scanning Patterns: Eyetracking Evidence" (2019); Nielsen Norman Group, Wang and Chan, "5 Formatting Techniques for Long-Form Content" (2023); Nielsen Norman Group, Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web" (1997) |
| C102 | An informational article on a site that sells related products mentions and links the product in the body text or directly at the end of the article, not only in the logo, header or a side margin, because visitors who arrive from search often never see them. Distinct from N18R, which places inline links to related items at the point of reading, but does not require articles to lead to the product itself. Does not apply to sites that do not sell the product; how visitors arrive is `NOT ASSESSABLE`. | Strong | Moderate | Nielsen Norman Group, Nielsen, "Informational Articles Must Ask For the Order" (2004) |

## How the product skill should apply these

Apply this file only when the reviewed evidence is text-heavy or web-rendered content — an article, a marketing/landing page, or another long-form informational page — where users scan rather than read every word. Do not apply it to short native UI copy (button labels, dialog text, single-line status messages), which is already covered by `core.md`'s C01-C06. Check C07-C08 together first: does the page front-load its conclusion and its keywords, or does a scanning user have to read past filler to find the point? Then check typography (C09-C10): is anything in ALL-CAPS or center/fully-justified that would slow reading? Then check C11 (numerals) and C12 (captions) as targeted opportunities — a page that spells out numbers or ships an image with no caption is not using two of the highest-value scanning anchors available. When the evidence permits measuring rendered or specified text width, also check C27: does the body text container hold roughly 50-70 characters per line, with a visibly proportional heading-to-body type scale? Report C27 as `NOT ASSESSABLE` when the evidence gives no basis to measure rendered text width (for example, a content description with no layout). As with `core.md`'s content rules, judge these against the page's actual primary goal (P01), not as a uniform style mandate independent of context.

Nielsen Norman Group research rules added in this file: C97, C98, C99, C100, C101, C102. Check each when the evidence shows its surface; each reports `NOT ASSESSABLE` for parts the evidence does not show.
